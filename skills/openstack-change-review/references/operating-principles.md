## Control and the four capabilities

- The platform is **under control** when the organization can understand its
  state, decide deliberately, intervene safely, recover and keep evolving
  without depending on individuals or undocumented assumptions. Availability is
  an outcome; control is a capability. [Book: Intro, Ch. 2]
- Four capabilities: **Visibility** (useful evidence, not more data),
  **Stability** (a trusted operating state, protected), **Organization**
  (capability that survives people leaving), **Governance** (decisions made with
  evidence, authority and accountability). [Book: Ch. 2]
- Control is relative to consequence: focus where low control meets high
  business impact. [Book: Ch. 2]
- Control must be **evidenced**, not felt. Confidence from experience is not
  proof that someone else could do the same under pressure. [Book: Ch. 2, Ch. 5]
- **Control debt** is distinct from technical debt: a customization known to
  three engineers is technical debt; the same customization known to one
  engineer, undocumented and essential to recovery, is *also* control debt.
  [Book: Ch. 3]

## The control loop

Observe → Understand → Prioritize → Decide → Act → Verify → Learn. [Book: Ch. 4]

- Light for routine work, more explicit as consequence and uncertainty grow.
- When incidents and maintenance consume all capacity, the loop quietly loses
  its last two steps, Verify and Learn; the quietest failure is learning that
  is not retained.
- "Technical success is not service success": a successful live migration does
  not prove the problem it was meant to solve is gone.
- The loop lets the decision change as evidence changes ("fix Nova" can become
  "protect the trusted state while accepting a loss of provisioning").

## Investigation

- A symptom is not a diagnosis; a request ("migrate these VMs", "upgrade this")
  is not a diagnosis either — translate it back into the problem first.
  [Book: Ch. 9]
- Separate **facts** (observed), **hypotheses** (explain facts) and
  **assumptions** (believed, not demonstrated). Try to disprove hypotheses, not
  only confirm them. Familiar fixes applied to a different failure mode add
  risk. [Book: Ch. 9]
- OpenStack symptoms routinely originate in another service or a shared
  dependency (message bus, database, network, storage). Follow the dependency
  path, not the service that raised the alarm. [Book: Ch. 5, Ch. 8]
- A running process is not a healthy service. [Book: Ch. 8]
- Ask **what changed**. In the author's experience it resolves more incidents
  than any single metric. [Book: Ch. 8]
- Make the experiment smaller than the platform: one host, one workload
  pattern, one dependency observed without changing anything else. [Book: Ch. 9]
- Evidence proportional to consequence: the burden of proof rises with blast
  radius and irreversibility. When uncertainty is high and waiting is cheap,
  collecting evidence is safer than changing the platform. [Book: Ch. 9]
- Investigation has diminishing returns: decide once enough is known, and state
  what remains unknown and why the residual uncertainty is acceptable.
  [Book: Ch. 9]

## Change

- Every change consumes finite **change capacity**: windows, engineers,
  migration headroom, attention. Its cost is blast radius, uncertainty,
  recoverability, dependencies, criticality and available capacity — not the
  number of commands. [Book: Ch. 1]
- Before changing: understand what you are risking, how you will detect
  failure and how you will recover if you are wrong. [Book: Ch. 9]
- **Do not stack uncertainty.** Changing OS, deployment tooling, network and
  OpenStack release together makes failures impossible to attribute. Caveat
  from the author: a platform far behind on unmaintained, vulnerable releases
  may have no stabilization other than the transformation itself.
  [Book: Ch. 7, Ch. 9]
- Verification and stop conditions are designed **before** execution.
  Approval alone does not make a decision controlled. [Book: Ch. 9]
- A change already under way creates no obligation to continue. Resumption
  after a stop is a new decision. [Book: Ch. 19]
- A new state must earn trust; it does not inherit the previous state's
  stability assumptions. [Book: Ch. 9, Ch. 14]
- Stability sometimes means rejecting technically valid work this month
  because recovery or capacity is insufficient — that is sequencing, not
  resistance. [Book: Ch. 9]

## Recovery and degradation

- The control plane and the data plane fail separately: stopping the APIs,
  message bus or databases does not stop running instances, but new state does
  not propagate. Verify the exact behaviour per backend before relying on it.
  [Book: Ch. 1]
- **Controlled degradation** — deliberately suspending a capability (e.g. new
  provisioning) to protect running workloads and platform state — is often
  safer than letting an unstable control plane keep accepting requests.
  [Book: Ch. 1, Ch. 19]
- Stopping is not giving up control. [Book: Ch. 19]
- Recovery is validated end to end on a business capability (e.g. create,
  reach and delete a test VM), not by process health. [Book: Ch. 10]
- **Fence before evacuating.** A missing heartbeat does not prove a host is
  dead. [Book: Ch. 10]
- Recovery is itself a change under pressure: reuse blast-radius control,
  monitoring, reversibility and stop conditions. Preserve evidence. [Book: Ch. 10]
- Repeated recovery is not stability. [Book: Ch. 9]

## Complexity, debt and lifecycle

- Complexity must earn its place: what value, who operates it, how is it
  recovered, what does it cost at the next upgrade, what happens if it is
  unavailable. [Book: Ch. 3, Ch. 15]
- Technical debt is not automatically repaid; retaining it is a decision with
  a reason, an owner, a review trigger and an exit condition. [Book: Ch. 15]
- A workaround is safe only while the risk it introduces remains smaller than
  the risk it removes. Repetition is evidence. [Book: Ch. 15]
- Stay as close to upstream as practical. A local backport is a risk decision
  with an owner, an origin, a test and an exit condition. [Book: Ch. 14]
- Automate execution before judgement (the author's stated position).
  [Book: Ch. 16]
- Security exposure is a control gap like any other. Security defines *what*
  must be achieved; capacity and business criticality determine *how safely*.
  [Book: Ch. 17, Ch. 20]

## Organization and governance

- Everyone can challenge. Someone must decide. [Book: Ch. 13]
- Expertise is not authority. Technical teams assess technical risk;
  authorized owners accept business risk. [Book: Ch. 13]
- Decision rights follow consequence and blast radius, and must be known
  **before** the incident. [Book: Ch. 13]
- Risk acceptance should expire. [Book: Ch. 13]
- Organizational boundaries must not become operational boundaries.
  [Book: Ch. 12]
- A procedure that only works when its author is present demonstrates the
  author's competence, not the organization's resilience. [Book: Ch. 10, Ch. 11]

## Questions to ask before any significant action

From the book's conclusion and Appendix E: What do we know? What remains
uncertain? What does the business depend on? What are we protecting? What
could this action break? Can we recover? Who decides? What are we accepting by
choosing this option? What will we learn from the outcome?
